[[By Flood and Fire - Ecologies of Home]]

#ecofeminism #geomythology #fireecology #ecologiesofhome #angelamanalanggloria #citrasasmita #nuestraseñora #womeninmythology #bicolmythology #aswang 

In the works of Balinese artist [[Citra Sasmita]], fire engulfs women. Tongues of flame orbit the figures as the conflagration coats their bodies, falling, most significantly, on the womb. In this geomythological conception of women, they exist with fire, their naked bodies coextensive with sacred flame while refusing sartorial regime. In her works, Sasmita challenges Balinese traditions codified in mythology and folklore by instilling women with agency marked by the feminine divine. In doing so, she articulates her own cosmology where women--sometimes depicted as snakes (*Fragments from Book of Fire 1, 2024*), other times as a totem of decapitated heads, each one with a spring of blood emerging from the mouth (*Peculiar Garden, 2022*)--embody the heroism historically attributed to males. The women of Sasmita's universe embrace the body as a vessel of personal memory and colonial violence, raising the corporeal as a force of nature. In an interview with KADIST, she states, **“A woman’s body is a record of the experiences and realities of life that women experience and how they live their lives both in nature and social construction.”**[^1]

Bicolano folklore begins with ~~fire~~ a woman. The volcano is a woman. The river is a woman. So too are the serpent[^2], the Mother, and the whore. In popular and folkloric imagination, women are recast as vessels of rage and care. And much like fire, woman is revered, desired, feared, and ultimately regulated. As with the violent histories that Sasmita wrestles with, dimensions of power shape these portrayals, as the female becomes the subject of masculine feuds and social judgment, all while enduring the weight of colonial projections.

==A cursory look at Bicolano mythology informs us that women exist as the lesser deities. The sky is governed by the male god Languiton, the water and the celestial ocean by Tubigan, the wind by Paros, fishing by Maguindang, destruction by Kalaon, and the forest by Okot. [^3] Gugurang, the supreme deity, is also male, as are the asog, or minsters of the cult who acted and dressed as women. They are distinguished from the baliana, women who were considered the “most shred, libertine, and seductive,” even while they officiated many rituals for the tribe. Curious too the baliana as an exorcist, particularly casting away the asuang that possessed the sick and brought disaster to the lands. The feast of Haliya, too, is performed by the baliana. This is meant to revere the goddess of the moon as she battled Bakonawa, the sky-serpent.[^4]==

==The female is the snake Oryol, daughter of Asuang, who uses her beauty to deceive and seduce men. The female is the buruka, a witch very much like the brujas. The female is the naga with their silver eel-like tails, not to be confused with the flesh-eating magindara who possessed colorful tails.[^5] Female beauty acts as a counterpart to her tragedy. [^6]==

**Asuang and the Stolen Flame**

In one of the myths of Mayon, fire becomes the object of desire for the greedy Asuang. Asuang steals the fire kept by Gugurang in the depths of Mayon Volcano. This comes after Asuang had asked Gugurang for a share of the fire. Gugurang, in anger, begins his lament and tirade against Asuang, exclaiming, "You! You lay god! Look at your ragged mountain and compare it to Mayon which is the most beautiful in the world." In response, Asuang begins sending down spirits to influence the people to do evil.

	"How dare you ask for my fire!" Gugurang thundered. The earth trembled. "Don't you know that when the fire in my seat is carried by hands such as yours the whole world will be set on fire?"
	
	"But I will be very careful," replied Asuang.
	
	"Be careful! I myself with all my power cannot handle it."
	
	"But how can you threaten the people with it?"
	
	"It is not my will that does it. It is someone else's that you or I do not know nor will ever know."
	
	"But the rainy days are coming and I need fire to make me warm in Mount Malinao."
	
	"Why," answered Gugurang, "you have lived there for many years and this is the first time that you have asked me for it, what will you use the fire for? Look at your people; they can live without it."
	
	"Well, it is time for you to give them fire now."
	
	"Give them fire!" burst Gugurang. The earth shook and the people were more afraid. But soon Gugurang quelled the commotion. Asuang himself was frightened. He never saw him that way before. "They are not fit to have it yet! They must make themselves worthy."
	
	"Well, am I not worthy?"
	
	"You! you lay god! Look at your ragged mountain and compare it to Mayon which is the most beautiful in the world."

Asuang, in the pantheon of Bicolano gods, is categorically defined as male. But I offer a speculative approach to the story where Asuang, as in the colonial demonification of the generic asuang, is read as female. After all, *tugang* (sibling) in the Bicolano language is asexual. In Ricky Lee's novel, [[Si Amapola sa 65 na Kabanata]], the author similarly engages in the queering of the asuang.

Read in this manner, the story of Asuang and Gugurang sends a strong message against (female) rebellion as underscored by the statements made by Gugurang. In the heat of their argument, Asuang challenges the rule of Gugurang. “But between us two, there is not much difference. Why must I live in a humble place like Mount Malinao while you sit here gloating over your power unlimited and unchecked?” As a child, it also never sit right with me that Gugurang's gloating and preoccupation with beauty is rarely called into question.

Everything culminates in the disfigurement of Mount Malinao, home of Asuang. A figure of abject horror, the withered and tragic Aswang typically functions as a foil to the beauty and gallantry of protagonists in media. Mythology positions the two gods as the opposing ends of good and evil and this is galvanized when Asuang ends up stealing the fire from the heart of Mayon. As they take the flame out of the volcano, the lands are covered in a blazing inferno. Gugurang pursues Asuang and eventually subdues Asuang to retrieve the fire. At the conclusion of this godly battle, the humans end up keeping some of the fallen embers. These kept them warm in the deluge of rain sent by Gugurang. It is in this way that Asuang, the rageful villain, inadvertently fulfills an act of kindness for the people of Albay. ==While Gugurang's fire functions as a disciplinary tool, Asuang's fire represents the rupture of the moral divide.==

Many read the aswang alongside its female nature, positing that she is female transmogrified, historically tracing the babaylan and the revolutionary scornfully recast as monstrous under colonial and patriarchal regimes. Herminia Meñez suggests that the perversion of asuang emerged as a colonial phenomenon during the Spanish era, one that sought to extinguish indigenous animism that often came in the form of the baliana or babaylan. In a 1983 text, Raul Pertierra ties the "asuangification" of women and the feminization of the asuang to the refusal to observe codified normality between sexes while still occupying spaces socially delegated to women. He writes, "The notion of the asuang represents a denial of basic sociality and a refusal to recognize reciprocity and exchange. Asuangs are dangerous not only because they deny the basis of society but also because they intrude into its innermost recesses." Thus, for friars to maintain moral order in their patriarchal society, the supplanting of female shamanistic powers and sexual force had to transpire in order to force her into a role of monstrosity.

In Philippine urban legends, Maria Labo represents a modern telling of this inversion. Maria Labo, the OFW-turned-aswang, emerged in the 1990s as a figure of  horror in Negros. Maria Labo seeks justice for the defiling of her body by violent men. Her story centers on the brutal wounding of the female body, but there too is the harm thrust upon her by society and circumstance. She starts out as an Overseas Filipino Worker who is cursed with the vampiric power of her dying employer. Upon her return, she begins to strongly crave for human flesh and eventually kills and cooks her two sons. Her husband later discovers the horrific scene and begins to slash at his wife with a bolo. Maria Labo escapes and is said to still carry a large gash across her face as she searches for her next meal.

Maria Labo's story offers clear caution to OFWs, but that the female is once more the subject of bodily violence reinforces the point. Eisa Jocson writes, “Maria Labo embodies female rage. She represents revolution and subversion, summoning us to break out of our subservient Mary-fied shell and unleash our transfigured rage. It is after all, captured in the meaning of her name, Maria Slashed; the task is to hack open Maria. Her wound is a portal to our depths and a crack to unleash what has been repressed.”[^6] Interestingly, it is her wound that empowers and completes Maria Labo. Like the fracturing of Asuang’s Mount Malinao and the transformation of Magayon, all are figures of injured landscapes and defiled bodies, articulating the connection between woman, myth, and nature.

The story of Asuang and Gugurang offers no kindness to the former, as the moral remains: female rebellion results in ruin. Asuang is an insubordinate thief who harms the balance that would rather go unquestioned. They are inferior and end up being even less, as their home, Mount Malinao, is cut in half. The disfigurement here is notable as the mountain functions as an extension of the deity’s body.

**Magayon, the Maiden**

The legend of Daragang Magayon follows a tropism that echoes these ideas. Magayon is placed at the center of the battle between Pangaronon, her lover, and Pagtuga, a scorned admirer. When Pangaronon dies, Magayon takes her own life. In the legend, Magayon stands as the nexus of love, battle, and grief. Magayon’s body, buried by her father Makusog, eventually rises from the ground as the Mayon Volcano.

In the Bicolano imagination, Mayon is a resplendent marker of abundance, beauty, and home. But Mayon is also powerful and volatile. She is both maiden and princess, unswayed by the laurels and gifts of her suitors, but eventually trapped in the trope of the grief-stricken lover. Magayon’s loves works against her: her father is abducted by a jealous Pagtuga, her lover perishes in a battle for her affection, and, in a world without Pangaronon, she swiftly takes a knife and stabs herself in the heart. In fearlessly choosing love, Magayon inevitably chooses death and bows against the loss of self-determination. Magayon is not the demonic asuang, but in her story, the female body is once more led to ruin. Her legend unfolds as mythical evidence of the connection between women and nature.

In his book entitled [[Caliban and the Witch]], Federici explores how the rise of capitalism required control of the land, and by extension, control of bodies. Typically female, this articulation of control was meant to strangle the processes of reproduction and gestures of care, which in the eyes of the capitalistic patriarchal society, were unproductive.  The control of female bodies, for Chris J. Cuomo writing in [[Feminism and Ecological Communities]], translates in modern-day society to the perceived gendering of intellectual disciplines. It is no surprise, he explains, that we find males dominating the domains of hard sciences and disciplines relating to the natural or material world.

Federici points out how women accused of witchcraft were reduced to the destruction of their bodies, particularly by fire. This is a suffering inflicted by the same patriarchal society that perceived women as sinful. Kathleen Nadeau makes a powerfully simple statement in her essay entitled [[Aswang and Other Kinds of Witches: A Comparative Analysis]]: "The real fear of the Europeans may be based on the fear of uncontrollable women." In the same article, Nadeau discusses the relegation of women, who once oversaw tribal spiritual wellbeing, to the domestic enclosure. This is further complicated by the gendered dynamic with the male husband, whose life existed publicly and whose actions always required reciprocity, often transpiring in the public realm. In contrast, the female refused reciprocity. In the occasion that it did happen, it does so privately or implicitly. Here, Nadeau draws out the similarities between the woman and the asuang. The latter, too, resists reciprocity. As a monstrous entity, the asuang exists as an asocial figure who completely denies all social relations.

Women’s proximity with nature was also scorned, though many accusations of witchcraft only emerged years later, when the capitalist system deemed natural and paganistic practices to be unproductive. Hence, it is the old woman who exists in a close relationship with nature and memory, who is often the victim of these accusations. Magayon, like the witch, interfaces with multiple conceptions of the female. She exists in a complex bond with nature, further complicated through the intervention of the patriarchy.

In fact, the connection here between woman is not mythic nor mystical, but patriarchal and systemic. As Warren (1994), Warren states that, “the exploitation of nature and animals is justified by feminizing them; the exploitation of women is justified by naturalizing them.” Fire, in the mythological and folkloric narratives discussed significantly embody both power and punishment. 

**Devotion and Disaster**

Elsewhere, in the iconography of Nuestra Señora de Salvacion, we find a different configuration of fire. Fire in this space surfaces in relation to divinity, but the story of this Marian image also allows us to further draw out the relationship between women and nature.

[[Nuestra Señora de Salvacion]], canonically crowned the patroness of Albay province on August 25, 1976, offers another permutation of the dynamic of fire and the female. This Marian image contains a curious iconography that some churches deem perturbing. The Virgin Mary carries the Child Jesus in her left arm while reaching out to save a man stumbling backward. Below the man is the devil’s head which, in many depictions, show the grotesque figure spewing fire from its mouth. On the other side of Mary, an a figure meant to be a repentant sinner offers up a basket full of flaming hearts. The Mother, the Child, the penitent, the rescued, and the demon – quite a large configuration of figures is called forth to narrate the story of Albay. It is not surprising that Albay would have a Marian image that offered salvation.

In a small poblacion in Albay, we find another curious Marian image that exists uniquely from other like images. The Nuestra Señora de los Desamparados, affectionately called OLA or Amaparos in communities of reverence in Manila, is emplaced and highly localized in Bacacay, Albay. The image shows the Virgin Mary carrying the Child Jesus in her left hand, as she stands on a cloud  lined with roses. The Bacacay Amparos does away with the usual twelve-starred halo of the Valencian iconography, and is instead clothed in her simpler Mediatrix blue-and-white garb. She reaches out her right arm to a group of three aetas, kneeling in prayer and looking up at her. Behind them are three individuals, Spanish officials who figure into the story of OLA’s apparition. Farther in the back, we find Mayon Volcano, smoke rising from its crater.

OLA is said to have appeared to the aeta community in the hills of Bonga, saddened by the “misery and abandon” in which they were living.[^8] Their council of elders later seek out Capitan Domingo to help consult with the Cura Parroco. Later, the Cura Parroco of Malinao, Padre Mariano B. Bañez, appears and reassures the Capitan and his people, “Doubtless the Gugurang has seen your sufferings and heard your cries for help.” Later, Padre Bañez, in consultation with Padre Mariano B. Bersola and his economo Padre Enrique Balana, contacted a local painter to create the portrait of the Virgin Mary. The artist, Quirino Sanchez, rendered the image in oil paints and toiled from February to March of 1901.

Fire is the language of destruction, and in the iconography of the two Marian images of Salvacion and Amparos, flame also exists as an offering. In particular, fire figures heavily into the Marian image of Salvacion, perhaps more than others. The image recalls the iconography of Nuestra Señora de la Lumen de Cainta. In a painting by Fernando Amorsolo, the fiery fanged demon adopts an even more monstrous face which stands in great contrast against the graceful disposition of de la Lumen.

The image of Salvacion was carved in the 1770s from the trunk of a felled tree in one of the estates of Don Siliverio Arcilla. Mariano Dakuba, a tenant, was tasked with clearing parts of Don Arcilla’s hacienda. In the process, Dakuba chopped down a large calpi tree. The tree never wilted and was soon regarded as miraculous. A local sculptor named Bagacumba was commissioned to carve religious images from this trunk. From this singular trunk, three sacred images were created, allowin for the embodiment and reverence of nature as material in these holy bodies: Our Lady of Salvation, Our Lady of Solitude, and St. Anthony of Padua. On August 25, 1776, Salvacion went to Joroan, which was once a vista of Buhi, Camarines Sur, upon the condition that a chapel would be built at the center of the barrio.[^9]

“On this view, connections between ‘woman’ and ‘nature’ exist because women are part of ‘nature,’ as are all humans, and the suppression and hatred of nature is played out in specific ways on women’s bodies, activities, and conceptual frameworks. These connections are relevant because both women and nature are categorically devalued, with their distinct and similar qualities.” (p. 7, Feminism and Ecological Communities) The story of Nuestra Señora de Salvacion simultaneously subscribes to and inverts this relationship. The calpi tree is chopped--a felling of the body, but miraculously does not rot. In this inexplicable space of the consecrated, Nuestra Señora de Salvacion draws out the feminine divine through natural and material means. Interestingly, we can locate similar mutilations of the female body in biblical narratives. In biblical history, the disfigurement (martyrdom) of female saints are notably grotesque: St. Wilgefortis grew a bear and was crucified by her father, St. Dearbhla plucked out her eyes to deter a suitor, St. Agatha of Sicily had her breasts cut off in an act of torture, St. Lucy’s eyes were gouged out, and St. Rose of Lima combated her own vanity by disfiguring her face with pepper and lime.

**The Poet**

Female passion and scorn are likewise captured in [[Angela Manalang-Gloria]]’s *Soledad* and *Revolt from Hymen*. Manalang-Gloria provides us with snapshots of muddied morals - the repulsive woman - leaning into imageries of the carceral and the violent. In both works, she refers to the shame thrust upon sacred female bodies ruined by love’s passion, consequently painting a picture of the contentious and highly restrictive social-moral duty of a woman in the poet’s milieu.

*Soledad*, published in The Philippines Herald Mid-Week Magazine in 1935, is a work cloaked in fire – firebrand, flame, hell – using the imagery of its behavior – burned, aureoled, blackened – as metaphors for the unholy ruination of the eponymous character. Soledad is a woman in love who chooses love over a life expected of her. In the poem, this love delivers Soledad to hell. But the poet also captures the bigoted condescension of the townspeople when stacked up against the sincerity of a girl’s love: “And seeing nothing but her blackened spires/ The town condemned this girl who loved too well.” In doing so, Manalang-Gloria offers the tensions between religion and passion, virtue and purity, the social and the personal, the patriarchal and the feminine, and delivers a critique of the agency and autonomy of the female body. Love offers condemnation, in the poem expressed through the violence of the morals that conflate the two. Fire is present in the intensity of passion, but also in the destruction of Soledad’s name. In the end, Soledad, like her name, remains alone.

*Revolt from Hymen*, published in 1940, intensifies this critique and further explores this matter of agency against the female body by adopting an arguably bolder approach to the subject matter. In the poem, Manalang-Gloria situates the speaker in the first person and confronts the injuries of marriage in the violence of marital rape. In the poem, desire evokes pain and disgust while loneliness (*soledad*) brings peace.

Stepping away from the personae in the works of Angela Manalang-Gloria, Niles Jordan Breis, in his essay, [[Kung Tawagin Siya’y Angela Buruka: Sa Alaala ni Angela Manalang-Gloria]], considers his complicated sentiments toward the poet.[^7] The poet, he relays, is remembered as a buruka, a repulsive witch, who is largely hated by Tabaqueños. She loudly chastises people from the window of her large house, the former site of Bell Smith and Co., from whom the poet had wittingly bought the property at a rate much lower than market price. Buruka, a more derogatory term of bruha or bruja, as the poet is colored in the essay as being hated by the people of Tabaco. She is a matapobre who pours the piss from her orinola onto people who come near the Bell Smith house. In the essay, Angela is known for her dislike of the ordinary worker, in one instance even rudely castigating a padyak driver for his stench.

Breis recalls a stern and derogatory remark made by his uncle, “Aristokrata ang hayup na burukang ‘yan. Tandaan mo na asul ang kaniyang dugo samantalang ang sa ‘yo ay pula lang. Matuto ka.” Later, as he forays into a life of poetry and prose, he is reprimanded by his teacher, “Tarantado ka palang bata ka! Pagka-buruka lang ni Angela ang alam mo. Gagaya ka pa sa mga mangmang dito sa atin na wala nang ginawa kundi libakin si Angela. Tanga!”

 Breis mentions reading the biography of Manalang-Gloria written by Edna Manlapaz but seemingly fails to see the events that led to the poet's becoming. That she lost her husband violently, that her child was lost from her for several months, that she never considered herself nor her works good enough, that her relationship with Celedonio was filled with both love and resentment, that her life was marked by series of departures – *from her*, and that with all this, she admits to the coldness she offers her own grandchildren.
 
 Humanizing Angela should not come at the cost of simply painting a small-town image of the poet mythologized as a witch. Women perhaps would benefit from not being subjected to these reductions and roles ascribed to them. Here I  make a case for the poet who is so despised. She was not a buruka, nor was she a great goddess of poetry. She simply was. But this world recalls even more vividly the faults of women, and perhaps that is the burden that is continually placed on our backs.

The correlation that is drawn between the solitude of woman and witchlore is a worthwhile phenomenon to examine. Manalang-Gloria, like the aswang in Nadeau's text, refuses social relations. Like Maria Labo, like Magayon, like the deity Asuang, her life is cast with tragedies, ultimately leading to this resistance. And like them, it is this that carries the poet into the realm of social vilification, which many times she had explored in her poetry.

In my conversation with the daughter of Angela Manalang-Gloria, she expresses how the poet was never fond of social gatherings. In the home, she was simply a mother and a grandmother who was fond of handing out candies to her grandchildren. “As a mother, she was a little bit militarian,” she states. She placed a prime on studying hard. One of her grandchildren once asked how one becomes a poet. She replied, it is talent with which you are born, subscribing to  the idea of the artist as genius. His lola, he shares, is remembered in awards – from the local government to the National Historical Commission of the Philippines (NHCP.) I ask, “do you think there is more work to be done?” He evades the question. Reflecting on this moment, I consider how there is no pitiful story here, nothing to romanticize. The family is welcoming of students, researchers, and even fans of Angela. They have donated most of the matriarch's items to the Ateneo Library for Women's Writings and are looking into funding for the restoration of the Bell Smith house. It is a long-drawn-out ordeal that might not even have a promised end and it is clear that the family is aware of that. They relay that they had requested support from the local government, but no movement can be seen at the site. Perhaps the pandemic too had stalled any intended project.

Today, a simple faded marker from the NHCP memorializes the house of Manalang-Gloria. The Bell Smith milling company is remembered, and the poet is merely mentioned in the margin of the memorial text. Everything fizzles out in the heat of the sleepy town and the life of Manalang-Gloria exists as one of many here in Albay.

The poet died shortly before I turned two years old. She exists in conversation and in the constellation of people who once knew or remembered her. Tangentially, as she exists in my mind, she also marginally persists in the communal memory of the city where this essay ends.

In the quotidian scenes of the market, one finds that many are women. Manning the market stall requires a woman. In one of my interviews, I meet a woman who was operating the stall with her partner. The two women moved and talked swiftly. The women of the Tabaco City Public Market occupy their pace. Some do so quietly, yielding to the undemanding pace of this coastal city, while others make up the chatter in the market.

My lola used to manage abaca production in the backyard of the family compound. She was born in 1928 in a small pueblo called Joroan. The daughter of two Chinese immigrants, my lola was a simple woman who was taught to be frugal as a child. She had married my lolo, the son of a lighthouse keeper from a small island off the coast of Ajuy, Iloilo. My lolo left home in his late teens, wanting to go to law school. He ended up in Tabaco where he met my lola. There, in a coastal town, a child of the sea and a child of the mountains met and got married. As my lolo worked in the town hall as the Election Registrar, my lola, in her days off from teaching at a nearby public elementary school, put their money in to the growing abaca business of the ==1950s==. The two needed income, after all, to send my their six children to school. As a family, they prayed to Nuestra Señora de Salvacion in the evenings and regularly went to church on Sundays.

In the business, my lola had people working for her, winding up the abaca in the backyard of the family compound that they had auspiciously bought from the Confesor family who had owned the entire parcel of land in Bombon. The business would not last long, as the abaca production in the entire Bicol region dwindled. I wonder sometimes if lola had known Angela Manalang-Gloria as Angela Buruka. Years later, the poet's granddaughter Evangeline would be known to me as my godmother.

The mythic forces that define female existence in society hum below the surface and suffuse our discussion. But here, we quietly intervene with the material concerns that do exist in Tabaco. The displacement of ecological knowledge echo the transformation of the figure of the woman. Here, she is not the volcano, a witch, a serpent, or a saint. She is simply that lady waiting for a sale after sittting in the market for three days without having earned a cent.
 
This situation is mirrored perhaps in how Albayanos approach the rest of the females in this essay. Nuestra Señora, while a figure of devotion and salvation, also sits in the most commonplace corners of the home. She occupies an almost ubiquitous presence for the people of Albay. Magayon, as the looming volcano or the mythical woman dwells in the same cognizance. Hers is a story recited almost lazily to those who would listen. Magayon's name is uttered like a friend's: familiar and near at hand. For the people, Magayon has never existed as a mythic being nor a grand figure. She simply exists, as women do. The story of Asuang and Gugurang, on the other hand, is not recited as often, but when it is, is done so in a similarly languid manner. The tethering of women to nature, to the violation of their bodies, and to the infernos that orbit their stories exist, but in the domestic space that encroaches upon the public realm, there lies the quiet resistance against disappearance.
